7 Questions to Ask About Water Heater Repair (Before You Say Yes)

Your water heater is making a new noise, the hot water runs out too fast, or there's a puddle where there shouldn't be. You're about to call someone — but how do you know you're getting a pro and not a parts-swapper? These are the questions to ask about water heater repair that separate real diagnosticians from amateurs. Ask them on the phone or when the tech arrives.

Start With the Basics: Age and Diagnosis

1. "How old is my unit, and does that change your recommendation?"

A pro starts with age, because age changes everything. A six-year-old tank with a failed thermostat is a repair; a fourteen-year-old tank with a rusty base is a replacement wearing a repair costume. If the tech doesn't ask the unit's age — or can't tell you why it matters — that's a red flag. The serial number on the tank encodes the manufacture date, and any experienced tech reads it on sight. Watch for the honest answer you want to hear: "This one's young enough to fix" or "At this age, a repair is throwing good money after bad."

2. "What's actually wrong — and can you show me?"

"Your thermocouple's bad" means nothing if you can't see it. A real pro will show you the failed part, the corroded anode rod, the sediment pouring out of the drain valve, or the scorch marks on the venting. This isn't about distrust — it's about understanding what you're paying for. Amateurs diagnose from the doorway; pros open panels, test components with a meter, and explain the failure in plain language. If the explanation sounds like a script or the tech gets vague when you ask "why," get a second opinion.

Dig Deeper: Parts and Safety

3. "When was the anode rod last checked?"

This single question filters out half the amateurs. The anode rod is the sacrificial component that keeps your tank from rusting from the inside — and it's the most neglected part in residential plumbing. If the tech looks blank or changes the subject, they don't do real water heater work. A pro will tell you the rod's condition, whether it's replaceable on your model, and how checking it every few years can double a tank's life. Bonus points if they mention that our local water chemistry eats rods faster than average.

4. "Is my venting (or electrical) up to current code?"

Water heaters don't operate in a vacuum — gas units need proper venting and combustion air; electric units need correct breaker sizing and wiring. An amateur swaps the tank and ignores the vent that's been backdrafting for years. A pro checks the vent run, the draft, the gas line sizing, and the expansion tank (required by code in our area and missing in half the homes we visit). This question also reveals whether the tech is thinking about your safety or just the invoice.

Talk Money: Repair Math and Warranty

5. "Repair or replace — what's the honest math?"

Ask for the numbers out loud: repair cost versus replacement cost, the unit's remaining expected life, and current efficiency. A good tech will walk you through it without pushing — "the repair is $300 and buys you maybe two years; a new unit is $1,800 with a six-year warranty and lower gas bills." The 50% rule is a decent shortcut: if the repair costs more than half of replacement and the unit is past mid-life, replace. What you don't want is pressure in either direction, just clear math.

6. "What warranty comes with this work?"

Parts warranties and labor warranties are different things, and amateurs blur them. Get both in writing: how long the part is covered, how long the labor is covered, and who honors it if something fails. A pro's answer is specific — "one year on labor, six years on the tank through the manufacturer." A vague "oh, it's all covered" is worth exactly nothing when the pilot light goes out in February.

Finish Strong: Permits and Cleanup

7. "Do you handle the permit and haul-away?"

In our area, water heater replacements generally require a permit and inspection — and the old tank doesn't vanish on its own. A legitimate shop pulls the permit, schedules the inspection, and hauls the old unit for proper disposal or recycling. If the quote is suspiciously cheap, check whether these are missing; unpermitted work can bite you at resale or with your insurance.
